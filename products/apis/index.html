The Context

The wound stalled. Then the billing rules changed.

Two separate problems land on the same clinic at the same time. A chronic wound that has not moved under standard care, and a 2026 skin-substitute billing environment that no longer tolerates loose documentation or billed wastage. APIS® is positioned against both.

Documentation threshold

≥ 4 weeks

SweetBio’s documentation guidance defines the chronic wound at four weeks or more, alongside documented failure of standard of care. That pairing — duration plus failed conservative management — is the spine of a defensible chart. Payer thresholds differ, and some require a defined, immediately preceding course of conservative care with documented failure to progress. Confirm your contractor’s threshold before relying on four weeks.

2026 billing reality

No wastage

SweetBio’s 2026 guide states that CMS no longer accepts wastage modifiers, that providers bill usage for the exact size of the wound — and never more than what was actually applied — and that JW and JZ modifiers are not permitted for skin substitutes. Only the applied portion is billed; discarded product is documented, not charged. Modifier and wastage edits are contractor- and payer-specific and change — verify current requirements before submission.

Operational fit

One sheet

Supplied as an all-in-one sheet that is ready to apply in minutes, trims or folds to the defect, and conforms to the wound bed. It resorbs as the wound progresses — removal is not necessary, so an application never generates a return visit purely to remove product.

Documentation thresholds above are SweetBio’s stated best practices, not a coverage guarantee. Coverage, medical necessity, and payment are determined by the payer. Coding and reimbursement decisions are the responsibility of the provider and subject to payer policies.

Composition

Three known ingredients. One engineered sheet.

APIS® is a wound management device comprised of a highly purified collagen derivative (gelatin), Manuka honey, and hydroxyapatite. SweetBio’s core technology, MX™, is what binds those materials into a single conformable sheet rather than a layered stack.

01 · Matrix

Collagen derivative

A highly purified collagen derivative — gelatin, porcine-derived. Gelatin is denatured collagen: the processing that purifies it also removes the native triple-helical structure that carries collagen’s integrin-binding and MMP-modulating behavior. The manufacturer describes it as a natural extracellular matrix protein beneficial for cell migration, adhesion, and proliferation; that is a manufacturer characterization, not a cleared performance claim.

02 · Honey

Manuka honey

100% UMF-certified New Zealand Manuka honey. The manufacturer’s literature describes antibacterial and anti-inflammatory properties for Manuka honey as a material; APIS® is not cleared as an antimicrobial and is contraindicated in infected or contaminated wound sites. UMF is a graded scale and the manufacturer publishes no UMF grade for this device, so the certification alone supports no statement about antibacterial potency.

03 · Mineral

Hydroxyapatite

Hydroxyapatite — a calcium-phosphate mineral. The manufacturer cites literature describing protein binding and healing via angiogenesis; that is material literature, not a cleared claim for this device. SweetBio’s resorption statement covers the device as a whole, and we have not seen resorption data specific to the mineral fraction — hydroxyapatite is used elsewhere as a bone void filler precisely because it persists. If residual mineral in a graft bed or a Mohs defect matters for your case, ask us and we will put the question to the manufacturer in writing.

04 · MX™

The synthesis

MX™ is SweetBio’s core technology — the deliberate reconstruction of those natural elements into one biomaterial designed around the demands of the healing process rather than around a single stage of it.

Read these as ingredient-level literature characterizations. APIS® is cleared as a wound covering that absorbs exudate and maintains a moist wound environment. No antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, or angiogenic performance claim is cleared for the finished device, and none should be made for it.

What the device is intended to do

The intended use stated in the Instructions for Use is specific and worth quoting to a payer verbatim: to cover and protect the wound, absorb wound exudate, and provide and maintain a moist wound environment.

  • Cover & protectAn all-in-one sheet placed directly onto the prepared wound bed and secured with a secondary dressing
  • Absorb exudateFor highly exudating wounds, pre-hydration may not be needed at all — the sheet takes up wound fluid
  • Maintain moistureProvides and maintains a moist wound environment across the dressing-change interval
  • ConformTrims or folds to the defect and naturally conforms to the wound bed; may expand slightly in length and width on hydration
  • ResorbBiodegradable and completely absorbable per the IFU — removal is not necessary, and adhered portions are never forcibly removed
  • Apply throughoutCan be applied from the onset of the wound and for its duration, reapplied as required at reassessment

Engineered across the closure cascade

SweetBio positions APIS® as supporting wound closure from the early stages of bacteria and inflammation through the late stages of proliferation and remodeling. In the manufacturer’s own literature that specific statement is cited only to references 5–7 — the bench and comparison papers. The clinical publications are cited to a separate claim. Read the cascade positioning as mechanism, not as clinical outcome.

  • ClinicalLower-extremity chronic wound experience, preliminary clinical evaluation, secondary intention healing, and a randomized pilot study in Mohs defects
  • MechanisticTwo explicitly in vitro — bacteria reduction capability, and inflammatory-marker and growth-factor secretion analysis — plus a preliminary direct comparison of inflammatory reduction and growth-factor production capability across three commercially available wound products
  • Not a claim of sterility of the woundIn vitro antibacterial capability is bench data. APIS® remains contraindicated in infection or contamination at the wound site

Required qualifier on the closure and pain claims

SweetBio’s 4–8 week wound closure and lower patient-reported pain in Mohs wounds statements carry this footnote in the manufacturer’s own literature, and it travels with them here: Based on clinical and in vitro data; claims have not been cleared by the FDA. The manufacturer’s footnote continues with a comparison against standard of care. That comparative statement is outside the clearance and we do not repeat it.

APIS® is cleared through the FDA 510(k) pathway — cleared, not approved — for the management of wounds as listed in the indications. The closure-rate and pain statements sit outside that clearance.

How we present this to a physician

Plainly. The clinical evidence for APIS® is a set of published evaluations and a randomized pilot study — not a large multi-center pivotal trial. Two of the three mechanistic papers are bench work. We will not hand you a slide that blurs the two, and we will not let a rep in your clinic do it either.

  • Clinical evaluationsRodriguez 2020 and 2023 report lower-extremity ulcer experience; McMurray 2021 reports secondary intention healing
  • One randomized studyArnaud 2023 is described by its own authors as a pilot study in Mohs surgical defects — treat it as hypothesis-generating
  • Bench work is bench workBacteria reduction, inflammatory markers, and growth-factor secretion were measured in vitro. That is mechanism, not outcome
  • The closure figure4–8 week closure across chronic and acute wounds is the manufacturer’s summary of the publication set, and carries the FDA qualifier above
  • The comparison paperThe 2022 direct comparison is described by its authors as preliminary and measures inflammatory reduction and growth-factor production capability, not patient outcomes — it establishes no superiority claim in patients

Ask us for the papers. We will send the reference list as published and let your team read it before a single unit is ordered. If the evidence does not fit your patient population, we would rather you use something else.

Why this case is worth a conversation

Not because of the speed. Because of the baseline. This was a neuropathic, vasculopathic heel ulcer in an obese diabetic patient that had already consumed a year of clinic visits and product spend without closing. That is the patient your schedule is full of — and the patient whose chart a payer scrutinizes hardest.

  • Failed standard of careTwelve months of documented non-resolution is exactly the predicate SweetBio’s own documentation guidance asks you to establish
  • Four applications over five visitsThis case reports four applications across five weeks. Application count is a clinical decision made at reassessment
  • Documented progressionImaging at day 0, week 1, week 2, and week 4 — measurable evidence of progress, which is a documentation best practice in its own right

What the published case does not report

The publication reports no perfusion testing and no offloading, in a patient with documented peripheral vascular disease and a heel ulcer. Both are prerequisites before any skin substitute goes onto a neuropathic or vasculopathic foot, and both are required by most MAC policies. Do not model a chart on this write-up.

One case is one case. A single reported patient is not an outcome rate and should not be sold as one. It is a reason to run a small, well-documented trial in your own clinic — which is exactly how we would structure the first order.

Case details and the patient’s reported history are as published in SweetBio®’s product literature (MKT-032 Rev4). Individual results vary. Nothing here establishes an expected time to closure for any other patient, and time-to-closure statements carry the FDA qualifier noted above.

Application Workflow

Five steps, per the IFU.

APIS® can be applied from the onset of the wound and for its duration. Follow the most recent procedures and best practices for wound management for preparation, application, and secondary dressing.

Step 01

Prepare the wound bed

Prepare per standard wound care protocols and debride if necessary. Do not apply until bleeding, infection, excessive exudate, and acute swelling are controlled.

Step 02

Select size and count

Measure the wound and select the appropriate size and number of APIS® devices. Maximum weekly usage is size-specific and differs for adults and children — the table is below.

Step 03

Hydrate, trim, place

Low or no exudate: hydrate both sides with sterile saline for up to 2 minutes, or until fully hydrated — it may expand slightly. Highly exudating: hydration may not be needed. Cut or fold to fit, then place directly onto the wound. Transient stinging or an increase in pain shortly after application is commonly reported with honey-containing dressings and is not by itself a sign of infection — tell the patient to expect it, and what would distinguish it from the infection signs in the precautions, or it generates calls and premature discontinuation.

Step 04

Secondary dressing

Cover and secure with a suitable secondary dressing to ensure adherence to the wound and to provide protection. APIS® is not a standalone dressing system.

Step 05

Reassess and reapply

Change the secondary dressing as needed per protocol and exudate level. Inspect for healing, adverse reactions, or infection and reapply APIS® as required. Removal is unnecessary — never forcibly remove adhered portions.

Condensed from APIS® Instructions for Use, IFU-011 rev2. This page is a summary for evaluation and does not replace the IFU. Read the full Instructions for Use, which accompany every unit, before first application.

Handling & storage

  • SterilizationGamma irradiation — remove from packaging using aseptic technique
  • StorageControlled room temperature, 15–25 °C (59–77 °F); outside that range performance cannot be guaranteed
  • Package integritySterile only if unopened and undamaged. Do not use if the seal is open or the package shows visual damage
  • Single useDo not re-sterilize. Opened and unused material must be disposed of
  • ExpirationDo not use after the expiration date stated on the product label and packaging
  • DisposalUnused, unopened product is not considered environmentally hazardous and, per the IFU, can be disposed of in regular household waste. In a clinic that language does not govern: product that has been in contact with a wound, and any dressing removed from a patient, is regulated medical waste under the OSHA bloodborne pathogens standard, your state’s medical-waste rules, and your facility’s policy

Room temperature, no thaw, no cold chain. Ambient storage inside a standard 15–25 °C range means the shelf in the clinic, not a freezer — and no thaw window to schedule around before a procedure.

The coverage figure is not a unit count

The maximum billable area for any SKU is its physical surface area — for APIS-16x16-2 that is 2.56 cm², not 3 cm². Applied in full, that device is 2 units, not 3. Bill only the square centimeters actually applied to the wound, and never more than the measured wound surface area. The manufacturer rounds its coverage figures to the nearest whole number in both directions — 2.56 up to 3, 6.25 down to 6 — which is fine as a sizing aid and wrong as a quantity on a claim.

Indications & Contraindications

Both lists, in full.

Under the supervision of a healthcare professional, APIS® is indicated for use in the management of wounds, including the nine wound types below. The contraindication list is shorter and matters more — three of the seven are ingredient sensitivities.

IFU indication list — reproduced verbatim; not a coverage statement

  • Full & partial thicknessBoth depths, as written in the IFU
  • Pressure ulcersStages I–IV
  • Venous stasis ulcersLower-extremity venous ulceration
  • Diabetic ulcersThe indication behind the published heel-ulcer case above
  • Traumatic woundsHealing by secondary intention
  • Surgical woundsIncluding the Mohs surgical defects studied in Arnaud 2023
  • Donor site woundsWritten as “donor sites” on the reimbursement guide
  • AbrasionsWritten as “abrasion” on the IFU
  • Surface woundsListed on both the IFU and the 2026 reimbursement guide

A label list is not a statement of medical necessity

A Stage I pressure injury is intact, non-blanchable skin with no wound bed — there is nothing for a resorbable sheet to be applied to, and billing A2010 plus an application code for one is a straightforward recoupment. In practice, application and coverage begin at partial- or full-thickness tissue loss. Abrasions and superficial surface wounds that will epithelialize under standard care are unlikely to meet medical-necessity criteria for a per-square-centimeter skin substitute.

The IFU’s “pressure ulcers, stages I–IV” wording is reproduced above as written; current nomenclature is “pressure injury, Stage 1–4.”

APIS® is not indicated for

  • Collagen sensitivityPatients with sensitivity to collagen and its derivatives
  • Porcine sensitivityPatients with sensitivity to porcine-derived products
  • Honey sensitivityPatients with sensitivity to honey
  • Third degree burnsFull-thickness thermal injury
  • InfectionInfections or contamination in the wound site
  • Bleeding controlControl of bleeding — APIS® is not a hemostat
  • Very small childrenChildren and infant patients under 10 kg / 22 lbs

Precautions and adverse reactions

Because of its composition, individuals with known sensitivity to collagen, porcine-derived materials, or honey may experience an allergic reaction. Remove APIS® and discontinue use if signs of sensitivity appear.

Do not apply until bleeding, infection, excessive exudate, and acute swelling are controlled. Contact a healthcare professional if signs of infection occur (for example increased pain), if wound color and/or odor changes, if the wound does not begin to show signs of healing, or if any other unexpected symptoms occur.

Federal (U.S.A.) law restricts this device to sale by or on the order of a licensed healthcare practitioner. Coding & Reimbursement

A2010 — per square centimeter.

An established HCPCS code, a defined CPT application ladder, and a 2026 rule set that punishes sloppy documentation. This is the part of the conversation we spend the most time on, and the part your billing team will judge us by.

Coverage before coding

CMS withdrew the DFU/VLU skin-substitute LCDs on 24 December 2025 — they never took effect. As of mid-2026, only Novitas (L35041), First Coast and CGS operate active skin-substitute LCDs. In every other jurisdiction, coverage is decided case by case under §1862(a)(1)(A), which puts the entire weight on the medical record. Where an LCD or LCA does apply, check the contractor’s covered-product article, the covered indication list, and any per-episode application limit before the first application. We verify the current posture for your jurisdiction and your product, per account.

MAC product listing and application limits. Most MAC skin-substitute policies enumerate covered products by name and cap the number of applications per wound per treatment episode. Confirm this product’s listing status and the application limit with your MAC before the first case — a product absent from the covered-product list is non-covered no matter how good the documentation. We will pull the current listing for your MAC on request.

How to bill in the physician office

Four mechanics from SweetBio’s 2026 reimbursement guide, with one correction of ours: use place of service 11; bill the CPT code or codes plus A2010 for the total square centimeters applied; combine wounds by anatomical location — which CPT permits only within the same anatomic code family, so sum areas within a grouping and report wounds in different groupings separately; and make sure every add-on code accompanies its primary.

Coding by anatomic site, then wound size

The anatomic site chooses the code family before the size chooses the row. Add-on codes are never reported without their primary, so every row below carries the primary it belongs to. Common questions

What clinicians ask about APIS®.

A wound management device comprised of a highly purified collagen derivative (gelatin), Manuka honey, and hydroxyapatite. The collagen derivative is porcine-derived gelatin — denatured collagen, not native extracellular-matrix collagen; the manufacturer describes it as a natural extracellular matrix protein beneficial for cell migration, adhesion, and proliferation, which is a manufacturer characterization rather than a cleared performance claim. The honey is 100% UMF-certified New Zealand Manuka; the manufacturer’s literature describes antibacterial and anti-inflammatory properties for Manuka honey as a material, and no UMF grade is published for this device, so the certification alone supports no statement about antibacterial potency. Hydroxyapatite is a calcium-phosphate mineral; the manufacturer cites literature describing protein binding and healing via angiogenesis. SweetBio’s MX™ technology is what combines them into a single conformable sheet. Read all three as ingredient-level literature characterizations: APIS® is cleared as a wound covering that absorbs exudate and maintains a moist wound environment, and no antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, or angiogenic performance claim is cleared for the finished device — which is also why it remains contraindicated in infected or contaminated wound sites.
No — it is FDA 510(k) cleared, which is a different thing and the distinction matters in a chart and in a claim. FDA’s 510(k) database carries two clearances for SweetBio: K222143 (APIS®/VERIS™, decided 13 October 2023) and K182725 (Apis, decided 31 May 2019), both found substantially equivalent, both under product code FRO — “Dressing, Wound, Drug,” unclassified. Clearance covers the intended use stated in the Instructions for Use: to cover and protect the wound, absorb wound exudate, and provide and maintain a moist wound environment, in the management of the listed wound types under the supervision of a healthcare professional. Marketing statements about 4–8 week closure and lower patient-reported pain in Mohs wounds sit outside that clearance and carry the manufacturer’s own footnote: based on clinical and in vitro data; claims have not been cleared by the FDA.

No. Infections or contamination in the wound site are a contraindication, and the precautions state that APIS® should not be applied until bleeding, infection, excessive exudate, and acute swelling are controlled. The in vitro antibacterial work on Manuka honey does not change that — it is bench data, not a clinical antimicrobial indication. The full contraindication list is: sensitivity to collagen and its derivatives, sensitivity to porcine-derived products, sensitivity to honey, third degree burns, infection or contamination in the wound site, control of bleeding, and children and infant patients under 10 kg / 22 lbs.

The IFU states a maximum weekly usage per size. For adults: 44 of the 1.6 × 1.6 cm (2.56 cm²), 18 of the 2.5 × 2.5 cm (6.25 cm²), 7 of the 4.0 × 4.0 cm (16.0 cm²), 4 of the 5.0 × 5.0 cm (25.0 cm²), and 2 of the 6.0 × 8.0 cm (48.0 cm²). For children above 10 kg / 22 lbs the corresponding figures are 23, 9, 3, 2, and 1. These are ceilings, not treatment plans — the diabetic heel ulcer case SweetBio publishes used four applications across five visits in five weeks.
The product is billed under HCPCS A2010 — APIS, per square centimeter, with the application billed under the CPT 15271–15278 families. The anatomic site chooses the family before the size chooses the code: trunk, arms and legs use 15271/+15272 and 15273/+15274; face, scalp, eyelids, mouth, neck, ears, orbits, genitalia, hands, feet and multiple digits use 15275/+15276 and 15277/+15278. In the physician office, use place of service 11, bill the CPT code or codes plus A2010 for the total square centimeters applied, sum wound areas only within the same anatomic grouping — wounds in different groupings are reported separately, each with its own primary — and pair every add-on code with its primary. What changed, as stated in SweetBio’s 2026 guide: CMS no longer accepts wastage modifiers, providers bill usage for the exact size of the wound and never more than what was actually applied, and JW and JZ modifiers are not permitted for skin substitutes. Only the applied portion is billed; discarded product is documented but not charged. That guide is a manufacturer document summarizing CMS policy — verify it against the CY2026 Physician Fee Schedule final rule and your MAC’s current instruction before submission. These mechanics assume the non-facility setting; hospital outpatient and ASC payment works differently.
Seven elements, per SweetBio’s documentation guidance: chronic wound of four weeks or more; failed standard of care; measurements as length × width × depth; infection status; whether debridement was performed; the product size applied; and evidence of progress. Add to that the amount used and the amount discarded — you document the discard even though you do not bill it. Add two more that SweetBio’s list omits and payers do not: a dated vascular assessment (ABI, TBI or toe pressures, plus revascularization status where relevant) before the first application, and offloading or compression documented as prescribed, in place and adherent. Both are required by most MAC skin-substitute policies and are among the most common reasons a claim is recouped. Our team reviews your note template against that list before the first application, because reconstructing it during a records request is a different and much worse exercise.
