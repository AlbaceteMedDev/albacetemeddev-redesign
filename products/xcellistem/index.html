XCelliStem™ Wound Powder. Two source tissues. One sterile particulate.

An extracellular matrix dressing sourced from two porcine tissues — lung and spleen — and supplied as a sterile, single-use topical powder. Manufactured by StemSys, LLC and FDA 510(k)-cleared in 2018 (K172593); billed under HCPCS A2004 per 1 mg. Albacete MedDev distributes XCelliStem with coding and documentation support for wound-care programs.

Composition

Sourced from two tissues, not one.

The two predicate powders in XCelliStem's own 510(k) each come from a single porcine tissue — small intestine submucosa and urinary bladder. StemSys sources its matrix from two organs, lung and spleen: the multi-tissue platform (MTP) design.

Source

Two organs, one matrix

Porcine lung and spleen, processed into a white to off-white particulate. The predicates in K172593 — Cook ECM Powder (K152033) and ACell Powder Wound Dressing (K060888) — derive from small intestine submucosa and urinary bladder respectively. Same product code, same particulate format, different source tissue.

Matrix profile

More than collagen

The 510(k) characterized the matrix's collagen types, elastin, fibronectin, laminin, glycosaminoglycan, hyaluronic-acid, and lipid composition. In manufacturer poster data, the split runs roughly 45% collagen, 25% elastin, 14% fibronectin, and 7% laminin — against ~90% collagen typical of single-tissue ECMs — plus hyaluronic acid, GAGs, and growth factors (FGF, VEGF, EGF). Read these as composition figures, not outcome claims.

Format

A powder that follows the wound bed

Applied topically, the particulate settles into irregular wound beds, including tunneled or undermined wounds — a wound type named on the cleared indication statement. Per the 510(k), the mode of action is covering the wound and maintaining a moist environment for wound management. Supplied sterile (E-beam), for one-time use.

Regulatory

The regulatory anchor is K172593.

XCelliStem is an FDA 510(k)-cleared wound dressing. On March 16, 2018, FDA determined it substantially equivalent to two legally marketed predicate powders. "Cleared" is the precise word — a 510(k) is not an approval, and we do not use the terms interchangeably.

Cleared intended use — quoted from the 510(k)

"The XCelliStem Wound Powder is intended for the management of wounds including: partial and full-thickness wounds, pressure ulcers, diabetic ulcers, venous ulcers, chronic vascular ulcers, tunneled/undermined wounds, surgical wounds (donor sites/grafts, post-Moh’s surgery, post-laser surgery, podiatric, wound dehiscence), trauma wounds (abrasions, lacerations, second-degree burns, and skin tears), and draining wounds. The device is intended for one-time use."

  • The statement is about wound management — covering the wound and maintaining a moist wound environment — not a healing-rate claim.
  • Chronic ulcer categories (pressure, diabetic, venous, chronic vascular) are on the cleared list; see the evidence section for where published outcomes actually sit.
  • Single-use device, supplied sterile, applied topically.
Evidence

The clinical file, graded honestly.

The published record on XCelliStem, by design and sample size. There are no randomized controlled trials, and a July 2026 ClinicalTrials.gov search returned zero registered trials. Across all published case series, case reports, and posters: roughly 30 identifiable patients, plus a 25-patient treated cohort in one retrospective comparison.

PubMed-indexed · case series

Irfan 2025 — n=15, ballistic and blast wounds

Retrospective case series at European Gaza Hospital (Antibiotics, 2025). Fifteen ballistic/blast injuries treated April–June 2024; 80% had exposed bone or tendon. Granulation tissue by day 7 in 12 of 15 wounds; closure in 13 of 15 by graft, flap, or secondary intention. No adverse reactions reported.

PubMed-indexed · case series

Khorsandi 2026 — n=2, free-flap salvage

Two tibial wounds after failed ALT free flaps (Plast Reconstr Surg Glob Open, 2026). Both wound beds granulated and accepted split-thickness skin grafts — at post-op day 16 and week 12 — with durable healing at 6 or more months and no major complications.

Retrospective comparative · not indexed

Creger 2025 — n=97, ventral hernia repair

Single-surgeon retrospective comparison in stage III ventral hernia repair: 72 cases without XCelliStem versus 25 with. Seroma and seroma-related complications fell from 49% (35/72) to 16% (4/25), p=0.0042. The only controlled comparison in the file — and the weakest methodologically: not PubMed-indexed, published in a low-tier journal, and a before/after design that cannot exclude concurrent changes in practice.

Read this before you cite it

The honest read: case-level support in surgical, trauma, and burn wounds — nothing in the chronic-ulcer populations that drive most A2004 coverage discussion.

  • No diabetic foot ulcer or venous leg ulcer study exists. If a payer or auditor asks for evidence in the indication being billed, the literature does not supply it for DFU or VLU.
  • The strongest-looking number — seroma complications 49% to 16%, p=0.0042 — comes from a retrospective, single-surgeon, before/after series in a non-indexed journal.
  • Supporting case work: Diaz-Valadez/Sasse 2023 reported 10 complex abdominal wounds, all healed without complication, median 2.5 weeks of treatment; two-week histology showed collagen deposition and neovascularization with no foreign-body granulomas. Not PubMed-indexed.
  • The only cost comparison in the file is materials-only: $520 per week versus $533 per week for negative-pressure wound therapy, from that same 10-patient series. It is not a healing-outcome comparison.
  • Posters add individual cases — burns, infected wounds (including an infected scalp wound with skull exposure, healed at 3 months), and one severe crush injury. Hypothesis-generating, not proof.

We would rather you know all of this before a payer does. Ask us for the full citation list — we share it as-is, gaps included.

FAQ

Common questions.

Straight answers on regulatory status, evidence, and billing.

Is XCelliStem FDA approved? +
No — it is FDA 510(k)-cleared, which is the accurate term. Under K172593 (March 16, 2018), FDA determined XCelliStem Wound Powder substantially equivalent to two predicate ECM powder dressings: Cook ECM Powder (K152033) and ACell Powder Wound Dressing (K060888). A 510(k) clearance is not a premarket approval, and we never describe it as one.
What does the clinical evidence actually show? +
Two PubMed-indexed case series: Irfan 2025 (n=15 ballistic and blast wounds; granulation by day 7 in 12 of 15, closure in 13 of 15) and Khorsandi 2026 (n=2 free-flap salvage; both grafted successfully, durable at 6+ months). One retrospective comparative study, Creger 2025 (n=97 ventral hernia repairs; seroma complications 49% to 16%, p=0.0042), which is not PubMed-indexed and comes from a single surgeon. The remainder is case reports and posters. There are no randomized controlled trials, and no study in diabetic foot or venous leg ulcers.
How is XCelliStem billed? +
Under HCPCS A2004, defined per 1 mg. Sizes are 250 mg, 500 mg, and 1,000 mg, single use. The code describes the product for billing purposes — it is not a coverage determination, and payment varies by payer. Albacete MedDev provides coding and documentation support; we do not promise coverage.
How is it different from other ECM powders? +
Source tissue. The predicate powders each derive from a single tissue — porcine small intestine submucosa or urinary bladder. XCelliStem derives from two: lung and spleen. Manufacturer poster data reports roughly 45% collagen, 25% elastin, 14% fibronectin, and 7% laminin, versus ~90% collagen typical of single-tissue ECMs. Per the 510(k), all three products share the same mode of action — covering the wound and maintaining a moist wound environment — and no head-to-head clinical study compares them.
Which wounds is it cleared for? +
The cleared intended-use statement covers partial- and full-thickness wounds; pressure, diabetic, venous, and chronic vascular ulcers; tunneled or undermined wounds; surgical wounds (donor sites/grafts, post-Moh’s, post-laser, podiatric, dehiscence); trauma wounds (abrasions, lacerations, second-degree burns, skin tears); and draining wounds — as a single-use device. One distinction worth keeping: clearance defines what it may be used to manage; the published outcome data sits in surgical, trauma, and burn wounds.
