The Problem

Not every wound is flat.

A tunneled abdominal dehiscence, an undermined sacral cavity, a blast wound with exposed tendon — none of these are flat. Tunneled and undermined wounds are named in the cleared indications, and a particulate can be delivered into cavitary recesses without cutting or layering a sheet. Whether that changes outcomes has not been studied comparatively.

Geometry

Cavities and tunnels

Tunneled and undermined wounds are named in the cleared indications for use. In the published abdominal series, three-dimensional cavitary recesses showed a fill-in response after powder application — the authors’ own description of ten cases.

Logistics

Dry, shelf-stable, no device

The Gaza case series selected an ECM powder specifically because it needs no electricity, no canisters and no trained device staff — the authors’ stated rationale for using it where negative pressure was intermittently unavailable.

Design intent

Built to be consumed

A particulate matrix is designed to degrade and resorb as the wound remodels rather than to persist as a covering — the stated design intent, and the reason the product is consumed rather than removed. Note carefully: infection is not among the cleared indications listed below.

Cleared Indications

What FDA actually cleared — word for word.

Reproduced verbatim from Form FDA 3881, Indications for Use, 510(k) number K172593. Nothing added, nothing softened.

Indications for Use — K172593

“The XCelliStem Wound Powder is intended for the management of wounds including: partial and full-thickness wounds, pressure ulcers, diabetic ulcers, venous ulcers, chronic vascular ulcers, tunneled/undermined wounds, surgical wounds (donor sites/grafts, post-Moh’s surgery, post-laser surgery, podiatric, wound dehiscence), trauma wounds (abrasions, lacerations, second-degree burns, and skin tears), and draining wounds. Cleared indication is not the same thing as published evidence

Diabetic ulcers and venous ulcers appear in the cleared indications above. No published study of XCelliStem exists in a diabetic-foot-ulcer or venous-leg-ulcer population. Those are two different facts and a reviewer will treat them as two different facts. The clearance establishes substantial equivalence to predicate wound powders; it is not a wound-healing outcomes trial. Read the evidence section below before you build a coverage argument on this product.

Two tissues, a different protein ratio.

What is established, and what is rationale

Keep the two apart when you present this product. One column of the ledger is documented in the 510(k); the other is a mechanistic argument drawn from the broader ECM literature.

  • DocumentedFDA reviewed device characterization covering collagen type composition, sulfated glycosaminoglycan, hyaluronic acid, lipid, elastin, fibronectin and laminin composition. The matrix contents were characterized as part of the submission.
  • DocumentedBiocompatibility across cytotoxicity, sensitization, intracutaneous reactivity, acute systemic toxicity, pyrogenicity, toxicology risk assessment, implantation and genotoxicity. Plus sterilization validation, viral inactivation, extractables & leachables, and bacterial endotoxin.
  • Documented, but read it correctlyThe “clinical testing” in the submission was a Repeat Insult Patch Test and a Skin Prick Test — irritation and sensitization endpoints. It was not a wound-healing trial.
  • RationalePublished discussion sections describe ECM scaffolds shifting macrophage polarity from a pro-inflammatory M1 toward a pro-regenerative M2 phenotype and releasing chemotactic cues for progenitor cells. That is scaffold-class literature, cited as background — not an outcome measured for this product.
  • Human tissue-level observationOne biopsy, taken two weeks after application in a single case within a ten-patient series, showed collagen deposition, neovascularization, fibroblasts and macrophages, and no foreign-body granulomas. One biopsy. Weigh it as such.

Percentage composition is reported on a manufacturer mechanism-of-action poster (Kimmel HM), not in a peer-reviewed analytical publication. The 510(k) substantial-equivalence comparison table lists collagen types I, III and IV for XCelliStem; Khorsandi J, et al. PRS Glob Open. 2026;14(7):e7950 and the Fortner poster list types I, III, IV and V. We report the discrepancy rather than picking the better number. Growth-factor content was not part of the FDA device characterization package, which covered collagen type, sulfated glycosaminoglycan, hyaluronic acid, lipid, elastin, fibronectin and laminin composition only; the growth-factor list traces to the manufacturer’s own material by way of a conference poster, not to a published assay, and has not been independently verified. XCelliStem contains no viable cells: it is an E-beam-sterilized extracellular matrix particulate regulated as a device, not a cellular or stem-cell product, and the product name does not denote cellular content.

How to say this without overclaiming

The 510(k) Summary — Section 5 of the submission, written by the applicant rather than by FDA — states that the subject device differs from the predicates in source tissue, that all three share the same mode of action in covering the wound and providing a moist environment for wound management, and that XCelliStem is as safe and effective and performs as well as the predicates. FDA’s own determination in the clearance letter is narrower: the device is substantially equivalent, for the indications for use stated in the enclosure, to legally marketed predicate devices. Either way it is a statement of equivalence — not superiority.

  • Defensible“It is sourced from two tissues rather than one, and the reported protein ratio differs accordingly.” That is compositional fact.
  • Defensible“It carries the same product code and the same cleared mode of action as the powders it was compared against.”
  • Not defensible“It outperforms SIS or urinary-bladder matrix.” No head-to-head clinical comparison against any other skin substitute exists anywhere in the published set.
  • Not defensible“FDA validated the healing claims.” The clearance rests on equivalence, biocompatibility, bench and characterization data — with irritation and sensitization testing as the clinical component.

Our position. We sell this product on what is documented: a cleared, characterized, single-use multi-tissue particulate with a real regulatory file and a thin but honest clinical literature. If a competitor’s evidence file is stronger for your patient population, we will tell you that.

Tier 1 — PubMed-indexed

Two papers. Both case series.

Neither has a comparator arm. Both authorship groups say so themselves, in print.

Irfan 2025 — ballistic and blast injuries, Gaza

Irfan B, Hamawy A, Musallam R, et al. Utilization of a Multi-Tissue Extracellular Matrix in Complex Wound Care in Gaza: A Case Series. Antibiotics. 2025;14(9):885.

  • DesignRetrospective observational, European Gaza Hospital, April–June 2024. n = 15 — 14 male, 1 female, ages 9–67, median 28.
  • Severity10 explosive-trauma, 5 gunshot. 12 of 15 (80%) had exposed bone or tendon at the time of application. 11 required two or more surgical debridements.
  • ResultGranulation tissue by day 7 in 12 of 15. Wound closure in 13 of 15 — 9 by skin graft or flap, 4 by secondary intention. Zero adverse reactions attributable to the ECM.
  • The confounder13 of the 15 patients also received concurrent vancomycin powder. Closure technique, number of applications and access to negative pressure all varied case to case.
  • Authors’ own limitationThe series has no contemporaneous non-ECM comparator, so outcomes cannot be attributed to the powder independently of debridement, antibiotics or natural healing. The authors write that findings should be read as associations under extreme constraint, not treatment effects.
  • Sponsor relationshipRTT Medical donated the product for humanitarian use, with no stated role in design, analysis or the decision to publish.

Khorsandi 2026 — salvage after free-flap failure

Khorsandi J, Olcott J, Iftekhar N, Franzoni D, MacDavid J. Novel Porcine Xenograft as a Salvage Strategy for Lower Extremity Wound Reconstruction Following Free Flap Failure. Plast Reconstr Surg Glob Open. 2026;14(7):e7950.

  • DesignCase series, n = 2. Two male patients, both after failed anterolateral thigh free flaps — one a proximal-third tibial wound after a grade III open fracture, one a left-foot wound following necrotizing soft-tissue infection. The abstract describes both as tibial; the case narratives do not. University of Nevada, Las Vegas.
  • MethodSurgical debridement followed by multilayered application of the particulate, then monitoring for granulation and delayed split-thickness skin grafting.
  • ResultBoth granulated and took split-thickness grafts — one at postoperative day 16, the other at week 12. One required serial applications. No major complications; durable healing and satisfactory function at a minimum of 6 months.
  • Scope the authors setThey state explicitly that the xenograft should not be considered before free tissue transfer for these defects — it does not provide vascularized coverage. It was evaluated only as a salvage-stage adjunct when repeat flap salvage was not feasible.
  • Authors’ own limitationTwo patients and no control group. They call for larger comparative studies to establish efficacy relative to traditional reconstructive options.
  • Sponsor relationshipThe study received partial funding support from RTT Medical.

What the design can and cannot support

The powder was placed along the anterior abdominal wall fascia beneath the adipocutaneous layer in a uniform layer, with drains placed similarly in both groups. The reported reduction is real as reported. The inference is where the weight is lost.

  • Single surgeon, before and afterThe paper describes a deliberate practice change. A before/after series from one surgeon cannot separate the product from everything else that changed with it — technique, patient selection, drain management, follow-up intensity.
  • Not indexed, low-tier journalA reviewer who cannot find the paper in PubMed will discount it, and is entitled to.
  • The limitations sectionIt reads, in full: “There are no limitations in this study.” Every retrospective study has limitations. That sentence is itself a quality signal — and not a good one.
  • What the authors do concedeTheir Future Directions section calls for larger samples and randomized controlled trials to elucidate the findings. Use that framing rather than the abstract’s.
  • How to use itAs a hypothesis-generating single-center observation in high-risk abdominal wall reconstruction. Not as proof of effect, and never as the sole support for a coverage or appeal argument.

Read this use carefully — a reported use, not a billable one

The cleared Indications for Use describe the management of wounds: topical application to a wound bed. In this series the particulate was placed prophylactically onto fascia beneath a closed adipocutaneous flap to reduce seroma, not applied to an open wound — and a stage III ventral hernia is by definition a contaminated field, while infection is not a cleared indication. This use is not within the cleared Indications for Use.

It is also not a use with a billing pathway. No skin-substitute application code (15271–15278) describes intraoperative placement beneath an adipocutaneous flap, and there is no established coverage pathway for it. Reported here as published literature only.

Everything else is a poster or a non-indexed case report.

Real patients, documented outcomes, no controls, and no peer review of the kind a payer recognizes. Useful for teaching what the product does in a room. Not evidence in an appeal.

The four gaps, before anyone else finds them

1. No DFU or VLU study exists. Diabetic and venous ulcers are cleared indications and they drive most coverage discussion for this code — and the published work is surgical, trauma and burn wounds. If a payer or an auditor asks for evidence in the indication being billed, this file does not supply it for the most common chronic-wound claims.

2. Total PubMed-indexed patient exposure is 17. Adding every non-indexed case report and poster brings it to roughly 30 identifiable patients, plus the 25-patient treated arm of the hernia series.

3. No comparative-effectiveness data against any other skin substitute. The only product-versus-product comparator anywhere in the set is negative pressure wound therapy, on weekly materials cost at a single institution — not on healing outcomes. The one control-armed study compares XCelliStem against no XCelliStem, not against another skin substitute.

4. The commercial sponsor appears in all three of the strongest reports. Product donation in the Gaza series, partial funding in the flap-salvage series, and an advisory-board relationship in the abdominal series. Each is disclosed in the publication. None invalidates the work; all of it should be stated up front rather than discovered later.

Evidence position current as of the July 2026 dossier review: PubMed searched for “XCelliStem” (2 hits, both above); ClinicalTrials.gov searched via API for XCelliStem, multi-tissue ECM and ECM powder (0 registered studies); manufacturer clinical-cases library reviewed in full. Application

How it is used in the published reports.

Described from the methods sections of the studies above. Follow the manufacturer’s instructions for use and your own institutional protocol — this is a summary of published practice, not a substitute for the IFU.

Step 01

Debride and measure

Irrigation and sharp debridement of devitalized tissue, then assessment of dimensions, depth, tunneling and recesses. The published wound series applied the powder to a debrided bed. Two reports sit outside that pattern and are worth knowing: in the hernia series the powder was laid into an elective surgical closure, and the lactational-abscess case was managed in the outpatient setting without formal surgical debridement. Read the hernia series carefully — prophylactic placement into a closed elective field is not within the cleared Indications for Use, which describe topical application to a wound bed.

Step 02

Apply the particulate

Applied topically and directly into the wound, including cavitary recesses. In abdominal wall reconstruction it was laid as a uniform layer on the fascia beneath the adipocutaneous plane; in flap-salvage cases it was applied in multiple layers.

Step 03

Moist cover, then dry

In the abdominal series the wound was covered with hydrogel or iodoform moist gauze followed by dry gauze, and left undisturbed for a week until office review.

Step 04

Reassess and reapply

Weekly or bi-weekly review in clinic; reapplication where the bed needs more. Reported cadences ranged from a single application to serial applications over weeks depending on wound response. “One-time use” on the cleared IFU is a single-use-device statement — opened product is not reused — not a limit on the number of applications to a wound. Payer policies do cap application counts; confirm your MAC’s limit before the first case.

Application coding and the unit basis

The product code is settled. What sits on either side of it — how the application is coded, and what a unit means to a given payer — is not, and both have to be resolved with the contractor before the first claim rather than after it.

  • Application codingA2004 is the product code; the application is a separate charge, and the code family is not settled for a particulate. Many MACs do not treat a wound powder as a “skin substitute graft” for CPT 15271–15278, in which case the application is billable only as debridement or E/M, or under the MAC’s own non-sheet application codes. Confirm with your MAC before the first claim.
  • Unit basisThe HCPCS long descriptor is “Xcellistem, 1 mg” — changed from “per square centimeter” effective 1 January 2022. Not every payer follows it: some commercial policies still describe A2004 per square centimeter, and CMS’s 2026 methodology for non-sheet products bases payment on documented wound surface area rather than on product mass.
  • No conversion existsThere is no published, validated milligram-to-square-centimeter conversion for this product. Document milligrams applied and total wound size in square centimeters on every date of service, and confirm the unit basis with each payer before the first claim.
  • Product listingConfirm that A2004 appears on your contractor’s covered-product list, and what its per-episode application limit is. At least one MAC’s published non-sheet product list does not include this code.

What to know about XCelliStem™.

XCelliStem Wound Powder is a sterile, single-use extracellular matrix particulate derived from porcine spleen and lung, supplied as a white to off-white powder for topical application. FDA cleared it under 510(k) K172593 on 16 March 2018 — product code KGN, unclassified, classification name “Dressing, Wound, Collagen.” The cleared Indications for Use read: “The XCelliStem Wound Powder is intended for the management of wounds including: partial and full-thickness wounds, pressure ulcers, diabetic ulcers, venous ulcers, chronic vascular ulcers, tunneled/undermined wounds, surgical wounds (donor sites/grafts, post-Moh’s surgery, post-laser surgery, podiatric, wound dehiscence), trauma wounds (abrasions, lacerations, second-degree burns, and skin tears), and draining wounds. The device is intended for one-time use.” It is cleared, not approved — a 510(k) is a substantial-equivalence determination. One point the name invites confusion about: XCelliStem contains no viable cells. It is an E-beam-sterilized extracellular matrix particulate regulated as a device, not a cellular or stem-cell product, and the product name does not denote cellular content.
It means the matrix is harvested from two source tissues — lung and spleen — rather than one. The predicate powders it was cleared against use single tissues: Cook ECM Powder from porcine small intestine submucosa (K152033) and ACell Powder Wound Dressing from porcine urinary bladder (K060888). The manufacturer’s mechanism poster reports the resulting matrix as approximately 45% collagen, 25% elastin, 14% fibronectin and 7% laminin, against roughly 90% collagen for single-tissue ECMs, with collagen types I/III/IV/V, hyaluronic acid, glycosaminoglycans and FGF, VEGF and EGF. Whether that ratio changes clinical outcomes is unproven — no head-to-head study against any other skin substitute exists. The 510(k) Summary, written by the applicant, states that all three devices share the same mode of action and that XCelliStem is as safe and effective as, and performs as well as, the predicates; FDA’s own determination was that the device is substantially equivalent to those predicates for the stated indications for use. Equivalence, not superiority. One qualifier on that list: growth-factor content was not part of the FDA device characterization package, which covered collagen type, sulfated glycosaminoglycan, hyaluronic acid, lipid, elastin, fibronectin and laminin composition only. The growth-factor list traces to the manufacturer’s own material by way of a conference poster, not to a published assay, and has not been independently verified.
