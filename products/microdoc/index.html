MicroDoc NPWT.

A canister-less, single-use negative pressure system with three selectable pressure settings — −125 mmHg for open wounds, −80 mmHg for closed incisions, −50 mmHg with grafts and biologics. One button, no canister, and no timer chip that ends therapy at seven days. Made by Pensar Medical of Houston, Texas; Albacete MedDev is an independent distributor.

The device

Single-use, canister-less, and it goes home on the patient.

Everything in this section is device handling, taken from Pensar Medical’s product literature and its application protocol. Clinical evidence is a separate question and is handled separately, below.

Single-use

Nothing to reprocess

Ordered by the unit as a complete system — pump, two dressings, Velcro strap and two skin prep pads — with the batteries already secured inside the device, so no one is fitting batteries at the point of application. It is single-use and is not carried from one patient to the next.

Portable

Belt, pocket or under clothing

Compact and lightweight, intended for mobile patients — Pensar describes it fitting on a belt, in a pocket or under clothing. A Velcro strap threads through the back slots so the cord tucks away. Applied by the clinical team, then handed to the patient to wear; the pump is not waterproof and is disconnected before showering.

Three settings

−50, −80, −125 mmHg

Pressure is selected by button press: −125 mmHg for open wounds, −80 mmHg for closed incisions, −50 mmHg with grafts and biologics. Pensar states MicroDoc is the only canister-less device that reaches −125 mmHg — that is the manufacturer’s claim, and we present it as theirs.

Three handling points Pensar draws attention to. Therapy is not ended by a timer chip — the device runs until the batteries lose power, and with a good seal using the UltraSeal strips Pensar reports therapy continuing past the seven-day window. Powering off takes a six-second hold of the blue button, which is there so an accidental bump cannot stop therapy without the patient realizing. And if the dressing develops a leak, the device cycles through its pressure settings to keep delivering negative pressure; if it cannot regain a seal it shuts off and restarts in roughly 40 minutes, with the dressing holding fluid safely until it is changed.

The evidence

Three pressure settings, and whose evidence they rest on.

Pensar offers three settings because the published literature on negative-pressure therapy points to different pressures for different wound types. Here is that literature, named — and a plain account of what it is.

−125 mmHg · Open wounds

The pressure described as the standard

Blume 2008 was a multicenter randomized controlled trial of vacuum-assisted closure against advanced moist wound therapy in diabetic foot ulcers. The 2011 International Multidisciplinary Consensus Recommendations address negative-pressure devices in traumatic wounds and reconstructive surgery; the manufacturer’s own summary applies that reference to closed incisions and traumatic wounds. The description of −125 mmHg as the standard for open wounds appears elsewhere in Pensar’s material as an uncited assertion, and we repeat it here as theirs, not as a cited finding. Chopra 2021 studied a negative-pressure incision management system after caesarean delivery in morbidly obese patients.

−80 mmHg · Closed incisions

Less force on a closed incision

Loveluck 2006 examined the mechanics of closed incisions under single-use negative pressure and reports lower lateral tension than at −125 mmHg. Watts 2024 is a systematic review and meta-analysis of two NPWT devices in surgical site infection; Horch 2025 compared two prophylactic single-use NPWT devices in a large US claims database.

−50 mmHg · Grafts & biologics

Enough to hold, not enough to shear

Evangelista & Kim 2013 (Wounds 25(4):89–93) examined the effect of varied pressure on skin graft incorporation. Sundef 2023 (OPTIMAC) is a multicenter retrospective observational study of tentative abdominal closure in the open abdomen.

What those studies reported

The figures below are the ones Pensar lists against each setting. They belong to the studies named above.

  • −125 mmHg43.3% complete ulcer closure versus 36.8% with standard moist dressings (p<0.007) in the diabetic-foot-ulcer trial; in the caesarean work, surgical site infection 8.0% versus 18.0% and dehiscence 8.0% versus 16.5%.
  • −80 mmHgComposite surgical site infection 24% versus 39% with standard care (RR 0.62, p=0.009) in the pooled surgical data, and lower lateral tension on the incision than −125 mmHg.
  • −50 mmHgSplit-thickness graft incorporation maintained while shearing force is minimized; and, in the open-abdomen study, 94.1% primary fascia closure (OR 8.62, p<0.001) with a lower complication rate than at −100 mmHg.

Not one of those numbers was generated with a MicroDoc device. Each was produced by the device the study actually used, in the population the study actually enrolled.

How to read it

Pensar heads this table with the claim that the studies show its three settings “deliver superior outcomes across wound types.” We will not put it to you that way.

Modality evidence, not device evidence

Every study listed is a study of negative-pressure therapy as a modality, or of another manufacturer’s device — vacuum-assisted closure, and the comparator devices in the caesarean, claims-database and meta-analysis work. None is a trial of MicroDoc. None compares MicroDoc to anything. No head-to-head trial of this device appears in the material we hold.

What the literature supports is the design decision: different wound types have been studied at different pressures, and a device that offers all three lets a clinician select the pressure the literature describes for the wound in front of them. That is the case for three settings. It is not evidence that this device outperforms another device, and results obtained with other devices do not transfer to it.

Common questions

What to know about MicroDoc.

MicroDoc is a single-use, canister-less negative pressure system. The ComforTech dressing seals over the wound and the pump draws sub-atmospheric pressure through a dome sited proximal to the incision rather than on it, so gravity cannot pull drainage into the port. Exudate is held in the dressing’s super-absorbent layer while the breathable film evaporates the water fraction — which is why the dressing must not be covered with anything occlusive. Pressure is set by button press at −50, −80 or −125 mmHg; Pensar’s protocol recommends starting at −80 mmHg, with a seal expected within three to five seconds. Powering off takes a six-second hold of the blue button. If the dressing leaks, the device cycles through its pressure settings to hold therapy; if it cannot regain a seal it shuts off and restarts in about 40 minutes, and the dressing goes on holding fluid safely until it is changed. Change the dressing when drainage pools under the dome or fluid gathers at the dressing edges.
Pensar’s own device comparison draws the boundary narrowly: closed incisional wounds, and shallow, low-exudate wounds that do not require fillers and have been assessed as appropriate for single-use NPWT. The same sheet states the device may be used with grafts. Deep or heavily draining wounds that need packing sit outside that description. Beyond it, what exists is case reporting rather than controlled data: Pensar publishes two single-patient reports — an abdominoplasty dehiscence in a 44-year-old, moved from a canister-based Pensar pump to the single-use system at −125 mmHg with a small foam dressing placed to fill the depth — a filler, which is outside the indication as the comparison sheet words it — reaching primary closure at day 86; and a non-healing venous leg ulcer with exposed bone in a 62-year-old with peripheral arterial disease, type 1 diabetes and hepatic cirrhosis, run at −50 mmHg, with epithelialization noted at the wound edges by the day-7 dressing change. Two patients reported by the manufacturer are two patients. Patient selection stays with the treating clinician, and the Operator’s Manual governs use.
