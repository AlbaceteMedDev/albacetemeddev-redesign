The Problem

A chronic wound asks for three different things — usually on three different days.

Bulk nonviable tissue has to come off. Bioburden has to come down and stay down between visits. And when a graft is the next step, the bed has to be ready to take it. Most clinics solve those with separate tools, separate visits, and separate skill sets.

What has to come off

Nonviable tissue

The cleared indication names it plainly: diseased tissue, necrotic tissue, slough and eschar, fibrin, tissue exudates, bacteria and other matter — in acute and chronic wounds and in burns.

What stays behind

Planktonic & biofilm bacteria

Mayo Clinic College of Medicine evaluated the QWTS in vitro against Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and Staphylococcus aureus. Their conclusion: the device eliminated planktonic bacteria and reduced biofilm bacteria. Laboratory work, not a clinical infection endpoint.

What comes next

A bed ready for graft

Preparing the wound bed for graft or other subsequent procedures is an explicit cleared indication — using contact and/or non-contact techniques. Arobella states it is the only company holding that indication for use.

Arobella reports the system has been used to treat chronic wounds, diabetic ulcers, burns, and other highly complex wounds. It is prescribed for a patient population of any age with one or more wounds, including patients who may also exhibit Diabetes Mellitus (DM).

Mechanism of Action

Focused ultrasound, delivered through fluid, from a curette you already know how to hold.

The hand piece begins as a curette — which is why it feels familiar the first time a clinician picks it up. What makes it different is a patented dome-shaped, sharp-edged probe that emits and focuses 35 kHz ultrasound off its face.

01 · Emit

Focused at the dome

The Qoustic Qurette’s dome shape is the emitter: ultrasound is generated by the AR1000B signal generator, carried to the hand piece, and focused off the curette face rather than radiated broadly.

02 · Cavitate

A stable cavitation field

The hand piece is calibrated to create a stable cavitation field. Held over the wound, that field helps separate necrotic tissue from healthy tissue — after which biofilm and necrotic tissue lift away easily.

03 · Couple

Fluid wash as the carrier

Sound travels better through liquid than air. The integrated fluid wash cleanses the work area and increases delivery of ultrasound waves into the bed — a capability Arobella holds under US Patent No. 8050752.

04 · Cut

The edge, under load

For denser tissue, the cutting edge of the curette takes over — and the ultrasound delivered through it facilitates the cut. The device is built to keep performing when the head is under significant load.

Cavitation is the formation of an empty space within a solid object or body. A cavitation field is invisible, but its evidence is not — bubbles form in the fluid well beyond the area the curette head physically touches. Arobella’s literature draws the bactericidal analogy to a wine glass: hit the right frequency and resonance causes the target to vibrate and then shatter.

How this sits alongside UltraMist®

These are two different jobs, not two bids for the same one. We carry both because a mature wound program usually needs both.

  • UltraMist®Non-contact, non-thermal low-frequency ultrasound delivered to the bed through a saline mist. A therapy device — it conditions tissue; it does not debride.
  • Qoustic QWTSNon-contact therapy plus contact sharp, excisional and surgical debridement, cleansing irrigation and lavage, and graft bed preparation.
  • The overlapBoth platforms sit in the non-contact low-frequency ultrasound space.
  • The divideThe Qoustic’s cleared indications include surgical, excisional and sharp-edge debridement, plus selective and non-selective contact dissection and fragmentation of soft and/or hard tissue. Confirm any other platform’s indications against its own 510(k).

Regulatory status — stated precisely

The Qoustic Wound Therapy System™ is FDA cleared under Section 510(k), not FDA approved. Arobella cites two clearances: K062544, dated January 3, 2007 and revised July 2, 2009, and K131096, dated May 16, 2014. Some Arobella-authored literature uses the word “approved”; the correct regulatory term for a 510(k) device is “cleared,” and that is the term to use in your own marketing, consent discussions, and payer correspondence.

Use outside the cleared indications is at the treating clinician’s discretion and responsibility. Nothing on this page is a statement of medical necessity for any individual patient.

In-Clinic Workflow

Hover first. Then cut what’s left.

Arobella describes the device as easy and intuitive to operate. The sequence below follows Arobella’s own product description — and it is the reason clinicians who already debride pick it up quickly.

Step 01

Set up the fluid wash

Disposable tubing carries the fluid wash to the tip of the Qoustic Qurette through the liquid orifice. The wash cleanses the work area and becomes the coupling medium for everything that follows.

Step 02

Hover over the wound

Begin non-contact. Keep 1 cm to 2 cm between the applicator tip and the tissue. Work a “mesh” or “spiral-out” pattern across the bed — light guidance contact is also acceptable.

Step 03

Let the field separate the planes

The stable cavitation field separates necrotic tissue from healthy tissue. Once that plane declares itself, biofilm and necrotic tissue are easy to remove.

Step 04

Convert to the edge

For substantive tissue, use the cutting edge of the curette. The ultrasound delivered through the curette facilitates the cut, and the device keeps performing under the load denser tissue puts on the head.

What this means for clinic throughput

Arobella’s treatment-time chart runs from wounds under 10 cm² through 200 cm², in 10 cm² bands. The banding is linear: every additional 40 cm² of treatment area adds one minute of ultrasound time.

  • Small woundsAnything up to 40 cm² is a one-minute ultrasound application.
  • Large woundsEven a 200 cm² wound is charted at five minutes of ultrasound time.
  • Room time ≠ ultrasound timeThese are ultrasound application times only. Set-up, positioning, sharp conversion, assessment and documentation sit on top.
  • Scheduling implicationIn Arobella’s chart, treatment time scales with wound area rather than wound difficulty — which makes the ultrasound portion of the visit straightforward to template.

Treatment times are the manufacturer’s guidance, not a protocol. Frequency, duration and modality for any individual patient are clinical decisions, and documentation must reflect what was actually performed.

Evidence

What the literature supports — and what it doesn’t.

The bibliography behind this device is real, but it is weighted toward in vitro work and trials that are now eleven to twenty-one years old. We would rather you hear that from us than discover it in a payer appeal.

In vitro · 2010

35 kHz against MRSA

Conner-Kerr et al. examined the effects of low-frequency ultrasound at 35 kHz on methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus in vitro. Ostomy Wound Management, 32–42. Laboratory work — not a clinical infection endpoint.

In vitro · 2015

Fibroblast morphology & migration

Conner-Kerr, Malpass and Steele examined 35 kHz low-frequency ultrasound applied in vitro on human fibroblast morphology and migration patterns. Ostomy Wound Management, 34–41. A mechanistic signal, not a healing-rate claim.

Randomized · 2005

Recalcitrant diabetic foot ulcers

Ennis et al. reported a randomized, double-blind, controlled, multicenter study of ultrasound therapy for recalcitrant diabetic foot ulcers. Ostomy Wound Therapy, 24–39. The strongest design in the set — and the oldest.

Also cited in Arobella’s bibliography

  • Karau et al., 2010In vitro activity of the Qoustic Wound Therapy System against planktonic and biofilm bacteria.
  • Serena et al., 2009Impact of noncontact, nonthermal, low-frequency ultrasound on bacterial counts in experimental and chronic wounds. Ostomy Wound Management, 22–30.
  • Gehling & Samies, 2007Retrospective chart review of noncontact, low-intensity, low-frequency therapeutic ultrasound on lower-extremity chronic wound pain.
  • Conner-Kerr et al., 2008Effects of low-frequency ultrasound delivered via the QWTS on neuroblastoma (SH-SY5Y) viability and morphology — the neuronal work behind the pain-reduction hypothesis.
  • Kavros et al., 2007 & 2008Noncontact low-frequency ultrasound in chronic foot and leg ulceration — a 51-patient analysis and a retrospective analysis.
  • Maher et al., 2014Low-frequency ultrasound for lower-leg ulcers due to chronic venous insufficiency — a report of two cases.

How to read this honestly

Three things are worth saying plainly before you build a program around this device.

  • Most of it is preclinicalThe bactericidal, fibroblast and neuronal findings are in vitro. They describe a plausible mechanism. They are not clinical proof of healing.
  • The controlled trial is oldThe 2005 Ennis multicenter RCT is the strongest study cited and it predates most modern DFU standard-of-care comparators.
  • The rest are case reportsThe internal case studies below are single-patient and small-series accounts written up by treating sites. Useful as illustration. Not evidence of effect size.
  • Bibliography breadth ≠ device-specific evidenceSeveral cited papers study low-frequency ultrasound generally, including other systems — not the QWTS specifically.

Case Studies

Three accounts, reported by the treating sites.

Reproduced from Arobella’s case-study appendix. These are individual patient reports, not controlled comparisons.

East Alabama Medical Center

Amputation closure

A diabetic patient whose left leg was amputated in 2012 required right-leg amputation two years later. His physician had planned the coming year as the healing duration — healing from the inside out takes longer but produces a better prosthetic attachment point. The QWTS was used to facilitate healing. After three months of treatments the patient was cleared to be fitted for a prosthetic — nine months ahead of schedule.

Graft bed preparation

Preparing a bed for Apligraf®

A demonstration that the wound bed can be prepared for Apligraf®, resulting in improved taking of the graft. Serial photography was recorded at Day 0, Day 14, Day 19 and Day 28. Arobella attributes the result to the combination of bactericidal effect and fluid wash conditioning the bed before application.

Limb salvage

A limb scheduled for amputation

Toes had already been removed for necrotic tissue and the limb was scheduled for amputation. QWTS was administered twice per week until healed, with serial photography carried through to Day 183. Reported as a difficult case in which the device aided the healing process and helped the patient salvage the limb.

Arobella’s appendix also reports a biofilm-removal series (Day 0, Day 1 post-debridement, Day 49 — skin pigmentation did not return, but the wound healed over); a two-patient chronic venous insufficiency series (a 63-year-old woman with two non-healing wounds over 12 months old and a 77-year-old man with seven non-healing wounds of three months’ duration, measured every 2–3 weeks, both showing improvement ranging from full healing to significant healing); a comparison study reporting a statistically significant increase in blood flow versus traditional sharp debridement; and two aerosolization studies reporting less splatter and aerosolization, and no contamination from aerosolization in the surrounding area. Three things that decide the code

Arobella’s quick reference lists codes but does not advise on selection. The following is our own guidance, not the manufacturer’s.

  • Deepest tissue removedSubcutaneous, muscle/fascia, or bone — the 11042–11047 family is selected by the deepest layer of tissue actually removed, not by the deepest layer visible in the wound.
  • Selective vs. non-selective97597/97598 describe selective debridement; 97602 describes non-selective removal without anesthesia. The two are not interchangeable.
  • Surface area treatedThe 11042–11047 and 97597/97598 families are area-based in 20 sq cm increments, with an add-on code for each additional 20 sq cm. The others are not: 97602 is per session, 97610 is per day, 15002/15003 run in 100 sq cm units, and 16020–16030 and 11000/11001 are scored by percentage of body surface. Measure and document the treated area — in the area-based families it is what drives code selection.
