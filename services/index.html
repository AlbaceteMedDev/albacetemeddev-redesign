Why one team

Products are the easy part. What breaks a practice is everything attached to them.

Choosing the graft, the dressing, or the device is usually the fastest decision of the day. What takes the time — and what quietly costs money — is everything downstream. How the encounter is coded. Whether the supply you opened made it onto the claim. Whether the note carries the documentation the payer wants before it will pay. Who answers when a post-payment review letter arrives long after the encounter is closed.

Bought separately, those three functions show up as three vendors who don’t talk to each other. Outside counsel starts from a blank slate, bills hourly, and responds in days. Generalist consultants borrow a deck from another specialty and leave before implementation touches the floor. And every revenue-cycle vendor promises more money — almost every one delivers it by adding work to your clinical team.

Run together, they are one operating layer. The advisor setting your coding posture, the analyst finding the supply that never made it onto a claim, and the attorney who will defend the chart are looking at the same documentation, for the same practice, at the same time. Nothing gets re-explained. Nothing falls between two vendors.

Where the handoffs happen

Six places the three services touch the same file. In a three-vendor arrangement each of these is a phone call, an intake form, and a week. Here it is an internal message.

  • A denial arrivesAdvisory triages it to the underlying driver — coding, documentation, prior-auth, benefit verification, or a payer-policy shift — and counsel drafts the appeal to the level of a demand letter.
  • A note falls shortRevenue cycle builds the template that captures the missing element inside your existing structure. Counsel already knows that documentation when a reviewer asks about it.
  • An audit landsPre-audit chart reviews have already run against OIG-aligned documentation standards, so the defense starts from a prepared record rather than a reconstruction.
  • A new service line opensAdvisory designs the reimbursement pathway and coverage posture; counsel vets the arrangement, structures the safe harbor, and reviews the contracts before signature.
  • The formulary changesAdvisory builds the portfolio around your patient population and payer mix — not around the deals a distributor is trying to clear. Revenue cycle checks what each substitution actually generates per encounter; whether you switch is your call.
  • Referrals driftRevenue cycle maps where local referral traffic is going — the PCPs, surgeons, and specialists in your market — and builds the relationships that redirect it.

Counsel works with your RCM team, not around it. The attorney who would defend the chart is in the room when the coding posture is set — which is the entire point of keeping the three under one roof.

Common questions

What to know about the services layer.

Are these services billed separately? +
Legal guidance is included in the partnership and is not billed hourly — there is no separate line for an audit response, a contract review, or a documentation question. Consultative advisory is likewise included, with advice and execution bundled rather than scoped as separate engagements. Revenue cycle management runs on performance-aligned economics — deliberately structured so our incentives match yours rather than billing you for activity. The specific arrangement is walked through in the opening 30-minute review, before anything starts.
What does the revenue-cycle analysis actually look at? +
Three steps. Portfolio analysis — a full audit of your current formulary, a service-rendering map, encounter-level revenue and margin, and a review of your existing workflow and EMR integration. Opportunity discovery — powered by AcuityMD, we compare that portfolio against peer practices in your specialty, geography, and patient mix. Integration — a substitution playbook and service-line integration built into your existing day, with portal-based tracking, monthly reviews, and quarterly deep-dives. Across specialties we consistently find eight recurring places revenue is left behind: services you could render but don’t, better-reimbursed product alternatives, bundled product-plus-service combinations, supplies used but not billed, undercoded encounters, referrals going to competitors, payer-coverage gaps, and documentation that blocks otherwise billable services.
Why does in-house counsel matter if we already have a law firm? +
Timing. By the time an outside firm is called, the documentation that was supposed to defend you is already written and the billing patterns are already in the record — and the firm is starting from scratch, billing hourly, and responding in days. Our counsel was there before any of it: already familiar with your documentation, payer mix, and clinical protocols, with a 24-hour response standard on audits, subpoenas, and patient matters that is often immediate. The posture is proactive daily guidance rather than reactive firefighting. Counsel works with your revenue-cycle team, not around it.
How much extra work lands on our clinical team? +
None — that is the operating premise, not a slogan. Zero workflow disruption: your clinical team does exactly what it already does. Our team owns the audits, the reviews, the recommendations, and the implementation. Product substitutions phase in without disrupting the workflow, new services slot into your existing patient flow, and documentation templates capture what payers need inside your current note structure, without adding words. Revenue capture should be invisible to the people generating it.
Is this only for wound care practices? +
No. The deepest hands-on experience is in wound care — 20+ years inside those practices — and that is what sharpened the instincts. The same playbook translates across every specialty that lives in the same payer-sensitive, documentation-heavy reality. The revenue-cycle framework is applied across 20+ specialties across the U.S., including podiatry, vascular, general and orthopedic surgery, plastic and reconstructive surgery, dermatology, pain management, interventional radiology, endocrinology, infectious disease, and hyperbaric medicine — in private practice, hospital systems, ambulatory surgery centers, skilled nursing and long-term acute care, home health, and mobile provider settings.
